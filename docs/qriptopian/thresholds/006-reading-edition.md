# From Constitutional AI to Trusted Intelligence

## Authority, Proof and Consequence in the Age of AI

*Reading Edition · Thresholds 006*

*Original paper: 30 August 2026. Reading Edition: 2 September 2026.*

Artificial intelligence has crossed an important threshold. For much of the past decade, the defining question was capability: what can these systems do, how quickly will they improve, and what happens when they exceed human performance? Increasingly, that question has been joined by another: how should powerful intelligence behave?

Constitutional AI is an important response. Anthropic's approach uses explicit principles to help shape a model's behavior, including processes of self-critique, revision and AI-mediated feedback. Its work on model values and collectively sourced principles recognizes something essential: intelligence cannot be governed by capability alone.

But there are two meanings of the word constitutional. One concerns the principles by which a system is constituted and behaves. The other concerns the legitimate exercise of power: who may act, for whom, under what authority, and with what means of challenge and remedy. These meanings are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Constitutional AI concerns the constitution of intelligence. Constitutional Computing concerns the constitutionalization of computational power.

That distinction matters most when AI moves from producing information to exercising agency. An assistant that offers an answer is one thing. An agent that transfers assets, changes production software, enters agreements, controls infrastructure or represents an institution is another. Its actions can change other people's lives.

At that threshold, good behavior remains necessary. It is no longer sufficient. We must ask not only how an intelligence should behave, but by what authority it acts and under what conditions it should be trusted to act. Those questions lead toward Trusted Intelligence.

## Ability is not authority

Consider a capable, ethical accountant. Their competence does not entitle them to transfer money from an account they do not control. A responsible physician does not acquire the right to disclose a stranger's medical records merely because their judgment is sound. An exceptional employee does not inherit the authority of the board because they might reach the same decision.

We recognize these distinctions readily in human institutions. Yet discussions of AI often blend capability, alignment and authorization as though they were different measures of the same thing. They are not. A model might be capable of an action, trained to behave safely, and correct about its likely benefits, while still having no legitimate authority to perform it. Alignment does not confer authority.

Authority must come from somewhere. If an agent acts for a person or institution, it must draw upon authority legitimately held and validly delegated. A human instruction alone does not settle the matter: the person giving it must possess the relevant authority and be entitled to delegate it.

A mandate makes that delegation specific. It establishes who may act, for what purpose, within which limits and for how long. It may limit further delegation or make permission revocable. Before an act proceeds, the system must be able to establish that the mandate still holds and that the act remains within it. Delegation cannot manufacture legitimacy downstream.

This is why a constitution for model behavior cannot, by itself, constitute a legitimate order of agency. It can help shape how an intelligence acts. It cannot alone establish whether that intelligence has the power to act at all.

## The person comes first

The constitutional starting point of this argument is the person, not the model. In the Constitutional Internet work on which this paper builds, personhood precedes accounts, credentials, platforms and computational representatives. A system does not create a person's personhood; it encounters a subject to whom its exercise of power must remain answerable.

This moves the problem beyond the internal behavior of any individual model. It concerns relationships among people, institutions, agents, data and infrastructures. Rights, standing, privacy, authority, delegation, accountability and remedy are not simply more alignment rules. They describe the terms on which actors can legitimately affect one another.

The need becomes clearer in a world of many models and providers. Different intelligences will carry different policies, capabilities and internal constitutions. They will interact with institutions, human principals and services that have obligations of their own. The constitutional question therefore exists between intelligences and the world, not merely inside intelligence.

Constitutional Computing names the proposed discipline of governing those relationships computationally. At network scale, the Constitutional Internet is its wider expression.

## From a claim to sufficient proof

An agent's assertion that it was authorized tells us little on its own. Consequential action requires evidence: who issued the mandate, what authority they held, what they delegated, whether it remained valid, and whether the action stayed within its scope.

Cryptography can help make those facts inspectable. It can support the authenticity and integrity of credentials, establish control of identifiers, and help preserve records against alteration. But it cannot create legitimacy. A signed illegitimate instruction remains illegitimate. A verified credential does not establish that its issuer was entitled to make the claim.

The distinction is crucial. Proof mechanisms can support a constitutional order; they cannot decide all of its underlying questions. Standards for verifiable credentials and decentralized identifiers offer useful machinery, not a complete account of legitimate authority.

Nor should proof require maximum disclosure. A system needs the facts material to a particular decision, not every fact about the people involved. Sufficient proof should allow authority to be established while preserving privacy wherever possible. The objective is to make important claims independently inspectable without turning accountability into indiscriminate exposure.

Evidence must also affect what happens. If a system proves that an agent lacks authority but allows the action to execute anyway, it has documented illegitimacy rather than governed it. Constitutional constraints need to operate at the point where an action becomes consequential.

That does not mean every decision must be a simple yes or no. A system might proceed, proceed subject to conditions, escalate for additional judgment, or refuse. What matters is that execution depends on the relevant authority, mandate, evidence and constraints, not solely on the model's internal impression that the act seems appropriate.

Even that is only the beginning. A system that stops observing at execution cannot know whether its assurance was sufficient. It must also look at what happened next.

## Seeing the consequence horizon

Actions produce consequences beyond their immediate completion. Some arrive slowly. Some affect people who never participated in the decision. Some are reversible; others close off options that cannot be recovered. A local action can spread through connected services, agents or institutions, and a seemingly modest action can become significant when repeated at scale.

Intelligence therefore needs to be consequence-aware as well as rule-aware and authority-aware. It must recognize that completing a task is not the same as understanding its effects.

The depth and breadth across which a system can anticipate, observe and attribute those effects is its consequence horizon. A narrow horizon sees the immediate result. A broader one asks who else may be affected, what dependencies may be created, which secondary effects may arise, and what might change when the same action is repeated thousands of times.

Consequence awareness comes before consequence calibration. A system cannot calibrate safeguards against effects it cannot perceive. As consequential capability grows, the horizon within which it reasons about effects must grow with it.

A low-impact, reversible act may require modest assurance. An irreversible act affecting many people may demand stronger authority evidence, independent verification, multiple authorization, simulation, monitoring or escalation to human judgment. Where possible, reversibility and remedy should be designed in rather than considered only after failure.

Operational Trust is therefore consequence-calibrated: the assurance required should be proportionate to the prospective effects of the act. This shares the context-sensitive outlook of established AI risk-management work, while adding the distinct constitutional question of whether the actor has legitimate authority to act at all.

The consequences observed afterward become new evidence. Once they can change the conditions of future action, governance becomes a feedback process.

## A constitution that learns from consequences

Cybernetics concerns control, communication and feedback. Constitutional Cybernetics applies that perspective to legitimate agency. The issue is not simply whether a system can adapt, but how observed consequences should affect trust, standing, mandates and constraints.

An agent can act legitimately within its mandate and still produce unexpected harm. If the system continues to grant identical permissions indefinitely because the original authorization was valid, it has failed to learn from experience.

Consequences must return into the decision process. They may reveal a weakness in the original model of risk, justify a narrower operating envelope, require stronger evidence, trigger review or establish a need for remedy. What happened yesterday should be capable of changing what the agent is trusted to do tomorrow.

This is the proposed constitutional feedback loop: authority and mandate bound an action; its consequences produce evidence; that evidence can revise trust and the conditions of subsequent action. The constitution does not merely constrain execution. It also responds to the world its actors help create.

That matters particularly when synthetic systems act faster and at greater scale than conventional governance can readily follow. A static rulebook cannot be assumed sufficient for a changing world. This is an architectural research direction, however, not a claim that classical cybernetics independently establishes the project's constitutional conclusions.

## Making trust operational

Trust is frequently invoked in AI but often left abstract. Reliability, safety, security, transparency, fairness and privacy all matter. Yet even a system with desirable general properties leaves a practical question unanswered: trusted enough to do what, under whose authority, with which evidence, and with what consequences?

A useful analogy comes from the distinction between Absolute Truth and Operational Truth. In *From Assertion to Canon*, Paper I of *The Polity Embodied*, the question is not how to settle truth for all time. It is what sufficiently established facts people and agents need to coordinate action, assign responsibility, resolve disputes and govern risk.

That work defines F.A.C.T. as Fundamentally Accurate Current Truth: an operational truth grounded in the best available evidence and interpretation, current to an identifiable state of knowledge, and sufficiently established to guide action. A FACT is a time-bound operational truth object, not a claim to metaphysical certainty. Its evidence, temporal context and route to revision matter. This is the project's epistemic formulation, not an empirically validated universal theory of truth.

Operational Trust makes a parallel move from belief to consequential agency. Operational Truth establishes a sufficient basis for belief and decision. Operational Trust establishes a sufficient basis for action.

Absolute trust would imply near-certainty that an actor or system will perform as expected across every relevant condition. Complex systems rarely offer that. Yet we board aircraft, undergo surgery, cross bridges and rely on electrical grids. We routinely depend on people and systems that we cannot inspect completely ourselves.

The practical question is whether conditions are sufficiently trustworthy for a particular consequential action to proceed. Operational Trust is sufficient, falsifiable confidence that an actor, system or interaction will remain within defined bounds under specified conditions. It is grounded in evidence, enforceable constraints, observable consequences and mechanisms of accountability or remedy.

Falsifiable is an important word here. A meaningful trust claim identifies not only its supporting evidence and operating limits, but also what would make us withdraw or revise it. Trust becomes something that can be tested rather than merely proclaimed.

There is a further distinction from operational truth. Trust can be supported through engineering as well as evidence. We can limit authority, narrow a mandate, introduce redundancy, monitor execution, require independent approval and stop an action when its evidentiary basis deteriorates. We can make some actions reversible and provide remedies for others.

Uncertainty does not disappear. But the conditions under which it can cause harm can be bounded through architecture and control. We do not have to rely solely on the intrinsic goodness of the intelligence.

## Why we board the aircraft

Most passengers know little about the particular aircraft they are boarding. They have not inspected its engines, audited its software, met its maintenance engineers or reviewed the day's weather models. Yet they entrust it with their lives.

Their trust rests on a system. Pilots are trained and licensed. Aircraft are certified. Maintenance is recorded. Operating limits are defined. Critical functions have redundancy. Incidents are reported, failures investigated and procedures changed. Licenses can be withdrawn and aircraft grounded. Evidence of consequence feeds back into future operation.

We do not make aviation trustworthy by searching for perfectly trustworthy pilots. We engineer the conditions around consequential action.

The analogy is not proof that any particular AI architecture is safe. It shows why model quality cannot be the whole trust architecture. A well-aligned model can be an important component, just as a competent pilot is. The larger system must still establish authority, evidence, constraints, accountability and the ability to respond when things go wrong.

The useful question is therefore not whether this AI is trustworthy in the abstract. It is whether this intelligence is sufficiently trustworthy to perform this act, under this authority, in this context, across this consequence horizon. More simply: trusted enough for what?

## What intelligence can do, and what it may do

AI is usually evaluated by its capabilities. As models improve, they can reason across larger problems, use more tools, write more sophisticated software and undertake longer chains of action. This is Raw Capability: what an intelligence is technically capable of doing.

Effective Capability is different. It is what that intelligence is presently authorized, sufficiently trusted and operationally enabled to do.

An agent might have the technical ability to transfer ten million dollars while being permitted to authorize only a fifty-dollar transaction. It might be capable of rewriting an entire production environment while being enabled to change only a development sandbox. Its intelligence has not been reduced. Its permitted scope of action is bounded.

Increasing Raw Capability must not automatically increase Effective Capability. Greater intelligence does not imply greater authority.

Authority establishes what could legitimately be delegated. A mandate specifies what has actually been delegated. Operational Trust concerns whether the evidence, environment and prospective consequences currently warrant its exercise. These conditions work together; none can simply substitute for another.

This offers a different response to accelerating AI capability. We need neither suppress every increase in technical ability nor automatically convert it into broader autonomy. We can allow intelligence to become more capable while governing when, where, why and to what extent its capabilities become consequential.

## Trusted Intelligence is not only AI

Behavioral alignment is part of Trusted Intelligence, but not its whole definition. An aligned actor may lack authority. An authorized actor may be unable to prove its mandate. An actor within its mandate may fail to anticipate serious consequences. An action may leave inadequate evidence, or its outcome may show that repeating it tomorrow would be unwise.

Trusted Intelligence addresses this larger condition of agency. It is intelligence whose consequential exercise can be made operationally trustworthy through verifiable authority, bounded mandate, sufficient evidence, enforceable constraints, consequence awareness, proportionate assurance and accountable feedback.

That applies to humans, synthetic agents, institutions and hybrid arrangements. The Invariant Research Lab's account of Hybrid Intelligence treats human and machine reasoning as complementary. Machine reasoning can contribute scale, consistency, synthesis and memory; human reasoning brings intent, purpose, contextual judgment, creativity and lived experience. Legitimate authority cannot be replaced by computational fluency.

The objective is mutual amplification, not a contest in which one form of intelligence makes the other constitutionally irrelevant. Trusted Intelligence describes the conditions under which their combined agency can be warranted.

## The question an index would have to answer

If Operational Trust helps determine when Raw Capability becomes Effective Capability, a computational system needs a way to evaluate whether those conditions have been met. This points toward the proposed Trusted Intelligence Index.

The Index is not fully specified here, and it is not a validated measurement system. Nor is it intended as a universal reputation score declaring a human or an AI to be a fixed percentage trustworthy. Such a score would lose the context on which the argument depends.

An evaluation must concern a particular capability, purpose, authority, mandate, environment and consequence horizon. It may need to consider reliability, epistemic integrity, authority provenance, evidence, execution constraints, reversibility, accountability and historical performance. A change in deployment conditions or evidence may change the answer.

How those dimensions should be weighted, what proofs they require and how the evaluation should be implemented remain questions for subsequent research. Existing project implementations provide partial enabling machinery, not independent proof of the overall thesis or a universal trust metric.

The proposition comes first: trust can be made bounded, falsifiable and evidentiary, and aspects of it can increasingly be evaluated computationally. It can therefore help determine whether a particular capability should be made effective. The Index begins with that question, not with a score.

## A constitutional order across difference

No single model provider can supply the Constitution for the entire computational world, nor should it. Models can retain their behavioral principles, providers their safety policies, institutions their governance requirements and jurisdictions their laws.

Constitutional Computing proposes another layer: a way to establish legitimate relationships among actors that need not be internally identical. Its concern is personhood and standing, authority and delegation, privacy and sufficient proof, mandate and execution, consequence and remedy. The Constitutional Internet is the network expression of that order.

Its purpose is to make legitimate consequential agency possible across difference. Feedback makes the order responsive, so that what its actors cause can change the terms of future action.

The aim is neither to create ever more powerful intelligence and simply hope that alignment will suffice, nor to prevent intelligence from becoming powerful. It is to allow power to become effective when legitimacy and Operational Trust warrant it, and to constrain, withhold or escalate it when they do not.

Ultimately, this is a project about human agency. As synthetic agency expands, people must remain meaningful constitutional subjects within the systems that act around and for them.

The progression is clear. Capability raised the problem of alignment. Agency now raises the problem of legitimacy. An intelligence that acts, transacts, represents, negotiates and allocates must be answerable not only for its behavior but for the authority and evidence under which its actions proceed.

The ambition is not an intelligence humanity is simply told to trust. It is an order in which intelligence can be demonstrably trusted for specified purposes; in which capability does not create authority; and in which the consequences of action can change what is warranted next.

Constitutional AI recognizes that increasingly capable intelligence needs principles. Trusted Intelligence asks what constitutional order governs the world in which that intelligence acts. Its most practical question remains: trusted enough for what?
